Scratch tickets vs lotto draws in Canada: how the odds and payouts really compare

Written by NextGen, British Columbia · Last updated November 2026

A scratch ticket says "overall odds 1 in 3.70". A Lotto 6/49 play says nothing like that, and its jackpot odds are 1 in nearly 14 million. It's tempting to conclude that scratch tickets are the better bet. This guide reads the official numbers behind both kinds of game, works through a full prize table line by line, and explains what "overall odds", "prize payout" and "prizes remaining" do and don't tell you.

Facts, not tips. We aren't recommending either type of ticket. On average, every lottery game returns less than it takes in, and nothing here changes that. If you play, set a budget you can afford to lose first.

Two different kinds of game

Draw games such as Lotto Max, Lotto 6/49 and Daily Grand sell you a play before a draw. Numbers are drawn later, and your play wins or loses against them. These national games are run through the Interprovincial Lottery Corporation. Our guide to who runs lotteries in Canada explains how.

Instant games, sold as scratch tickets or "Scratch & Win", are different. A ticket is a finished game when you buy it. You scratch to reveal whether it won. WCLC's FAQ says a retailer can't complete validation until a ticket is scratched, and its Prizes Remaining page adds that "the winning or non-winning status of a ticket - and the ability to claim a prize - does not change if you make a mistake when scratching it."

That difference shapes how each game publishes its numbers. A draw game has a fixed number of possible combinations, so its odds can be counted. An instant game has a fixed number of tickets, so its odds are set by how many prizes are in the print run.

Reading "overall odds" on a scratch ticket

Every instant ticket carries an overall-odds figure. Sask Lotteries, which lists WCLC's tickets for Saskatchewan, shows it right at the top of each game page. For example, the $2 Barnyard Blitz page (game 25421) says "Win up to $25,000", "Win Up To 5 Times!" and "Overall odds 1 in 3.70". The $10 $1,000,000 Ultra (game 16130) says "Win up to $1,000,000!", "Over $74 Million in prizes!" and also "Overall odds 1 in 3.70".

Two different tickets with the same figure show something worth noticing. The overall odds say how often a ticket wins anything. They don't say how much. A $2 ticket and a $10 ticket can both be 1 in 3.70, and the prizes behind that number can be very different. To see what the figure is made of, you need the full prize table.

Each page also states the claim deadline. Barnyard Blitz says prizes must be claimed on or before September 30, 2027. The $1,000,000 Ultra page says January 28, 2028. Unlike lotto tickets, which WCLC says expire one year after the draw, scratch tickets have their own date printed on the back.

The sheet also states: overall odds of winning 1 in 2.52; total prizes available 1,208,151; tickets received 3,050,000; prize payout 59.89 per cent.

What the table shows

  • Where "1 in 2.52" comes from. Dividing 3,050,000 tickets by 1,208,151 prizes gives 2.52. The overall odds are simply tickets per prize. The prize counts in the table add up to the sheet's 1,208,151 total, which includes the free-ticket prizes.
  • Many "wins" return only the stake. 469,640 tickets win $1, the price of the ticket. That is about 38.9% of all winning tickets, and about 15.4% of all tickets printed. Another 274,504 tickets, about 9.0% of the print run, win a free ticket. Only about 15.2% of tickets win a cash prize bigger than $1, which works out to roughly 1 in 6.6. So "1 in 2.52 to win" and "1 in 6.6 to come out ahead on cash" describe the same game.
  • The big prizes are rare. The three prizes of $100 a week for life are 1 in 1,016,666.67. Dividing 3,050,000 by that figure gives 3, so the odds are just the print run divided by the number of such prizes.
  • Prizes are fixed numbers. Winning counts are set per game. They don't grow or shrink with sales, unlike a lotto jackpot.

Two further notes from the sheet. It warns that "the quantity of tickets ordered and/or distributed may vary and could affect the published odds/prizes," and that "at the time of purchase, some of the prizes offered may have already been claimed." The same sentence about claimed prizes appears on the Sask Lotteries pages for current games.

What "prize payout" means

OLG's fact sheet defines it: "Prize Payout is the theoretical payout percentage based on the dollar amount of all cash prizes (excluding the value of free tickets) offered within an INSTANT Lottery Game at the time that the Game is launched, and assuming all tickets for that Game are purchased... and all cash prizes are claimed." It adds that the final payout is set when the game ends, based on the tickets actually bought with cash and the prizes actually claimed.

For Cash for Life, the stated payout is 59.89%. On a $1 ticket, that means that across the whole print run, the cash prizes add up to about 59.89 cents per dollar of tickets. Our check: adding up the listed cash prizes, apart from the three life prizes, gives $1,624,082, or about 53.2% of the $3,050,000 the tickets cost. The sheet doesn't say how the three weekly-for-life prizes are valued. The rest of the 59.89% presumably comes from that valuation, but that is our inference, not something the sheet states.

What the number means for one person is less obvious. A 59.89% payout doesn't mean you get back 59.89 cents on every ticket. Most tickets return nothing or the stake, and a few return much more. The payout figure is an average over all tickets, and a person who buys a handful of tickets can land far above or below it. The same average has a second side: about 40 cents of every dollar doesn't come back as cash prizes.

Not every corporation prints a payout figure on its pages, and the ones that do may define it differently. WCLC's FAQ gives a broader, all-products figure instead: of every dollar spent on tickets in its region, approximately 52.3 percent goes back to players as prizes. It doesn't split instant from draw games. We didn't find a per-game payout on an official page for the national draw games, so we don't state one.

Why "better odds" isn't the same as "better value"

  • Prize sizes differ. A scratch ticket wins more often partly because its prizes are small, and often equal to the price. A draw game's "any prize" figure includes a Free Play at the lowest tier. Neither "any prize" number is a chance to make money.
  • The top prizes aren't comparable. A scratch game's top prize is a fixed amount, from the thousands to the millions. A Lotto Max jackpot starts at $10 million, but the chance of winning it is far lower.
  • Per-dollar, not per-ticket. A $1 scratch ticket and a $6 Lotto Max play aren't the same purchase. To compare, spend the same amount: $6 buys six $1 tickets, each with its own chance at that game's prizes.
  • The odds don't depend on your choices. You can't choose a better scratch ticket by look or number, and for draws, every combination is as likely as any other. See hot and cold numbers explained.

"Prizes remaining": what the list can and can't tell you

Instant games have one feature draw games don't: corporations publish which top prizes are still unclaimed. WCLC's "Scratch & Win Ticket Spotlight - Prizes Remaining" page (last updated November 12, 2026 when we read it) says it lists "prizes of $100 or more that have not yet been claimed on tickets currently in the market or that have not yet expired." It notes that:

  • Some tickets on the list "may no longer be available at retail."
  • A "release date" shows when distribution began, and "availability at individual retailers may vary."
  • Games marked with an asterisk are sold in all participating Canadian lottery jurisdictions, and the list for them covers prizes unclaimed in all those jurisdictions, "based on information provided to WCLC."

As an illustration, on November 12, 2026 the page listed the $100 Elite game (62005), released June 1, 2026, with 789 prizes of $1,000, 9 of $5,000, 15 of $10,000, 2 of $100,000, 5 of $1,000,000 and 5 of $10,000,000 remaining. It listed the $100 Luxe Multiplier game (62004), released June 17, 2025, with 0 remaining at $1,000,000, $100,000 and $50,000, and 29 prizes of $1,000 still unclaimed. These counts change as prizes are claimed, so check the live page rather than relying on ours.

Limits of the list

  • It counts prizes, not tickets. The page doesn't say how many tickets are left in stores or in the print run. A top prize "remaining" may be on a ticket someone bought and hasn't checked, one thrown away, or one still in a pack that hasn't reached a store.
  • It doesn't change the published odds. OLG's sheet fixes odds at launch and warns some prizes may be gone at purchase. A ticket's printed odds stay the same whether its top prizes are claimed or not.
  • It starts at a threshold. The small prizes, which make up nearly all winners, aren't listed.
  • Thresholds differ by page. WCLC's list starts at $100. Other corporations' lists may use different cut-offs, so read each page's description.

Unlike a lotto jackpot, an instant game's top prize doesn't roll over. When the last top prize is claimed, it's gone. That is a fact about how the game is built, not a signal about what to buy.

Handling a scratch ticket

  • Check it before you throw it out. WCLC advises using the self-service ticket checker or asking a retailer, and notes that scratching mistakes don't change a ticket's status.
  • Ignore the control number. WCLC's FAQ explains that the "CNTL. NO." is a four-digit validation number under the latex and "can't be used to win a prize."
  • Mind the expiry date. BCLC says instant ticket prizes must be claimed before the expiry date written on the ticket or announced by BCLC. WCLC says the same for scratch tickets, which have an expiry printed on the back.
  • Claim through the right region. The Sask Lotteries page for a national game says its tickets "can only be validated or checked within the region they were purchased." Our guide to claiming a prize by province has the steps.
  • Treat unexpected winning notices with suspicion. See lottery scams in Canada.

A budget view

Because payouts under 100% mean an average loss, the useful question is the amount you're comfortable spending, not which ticket to buy. Using the Cash for Life figures, a payout of 59.89% means an average shortfall of about 40 cents per dollar over the full print run. A person spending $10 a week, or $520 a year, would on that average lose roughly $208 a year before any lucky run. Real results vary widely around that. WCLC's own responsible-play message is: "Hope to win; expect to lose" is a healthy attitude. Our responsible-play guide lists limits and support services.

Quick answers

  • Are scratch tickets easier to win than Lotto 6/49? They pay something more often, but the prizes are much smaller. They don't make winning money more likely in any meaningful way.
  • What does "overall odds 1 in 3.70" include? Every prize in the game, including prizes equal to the ticket price and free tickets.
  • What is prize payout? The theoretical share of ticket sales returned as cash prizes if every ticket sold and every prize were claimed.
  • Do the odds improve once top prizes are gone? Not according to the published figures, which are set when the game launches.
  • Can I pick a lucky ticket? There is nothing published that supports it. A ticket's odds are the game's overall odds, and a ticket's status doesn't change with how you scratch it.
