How Canadian lottery draws are run, audited and checked

Written by NextGen, British Columbia · Last updated November 2026

Every Lotto Max, Lotto 6/49 and Daily Grand result comes from software, not a drum of balls, and independent auditors watch the process. This guide sets out what the lottery corporations publish about who runs the draws, what the auditors do, how Quebec's law treats draw methods, and where the public information stops. It also separates two questions people often mix up: whether the draw is random, and whether the prize reaches the right person.

What this guide is. A plain-language summary of the corporations' own statements and rules. We have no inside access and haven't audited anything ourselves. None of this changes your odds, and no method of picking numbers beats a fair draw. See hot and cold numbers explained.

Two different integrity questions

People who distrust a lottery usually mean one of two things.

  1. Is the draw fair? Are the winning numbers really random, and could anyone interfere with them?
  2. Is the prize paid properly? Does a winning ticket go to its rightful holder, and could an insider claim it?

Different controls answer each one. Draw controls include certified software, independent auditors and a separate record of every ticket. Claim controls include ID checks and publicising winners. This guide covers the first in detail and touches on the second at the end. For the claim side, see our claiming guide and the guide to the first 30 days after a win.

Who conducts the draws

BCLC explains that it and four other provincial lottery jurisdictions make up the Interprovincial Lottery Corporation (ILC), which operates the national draw games Lotto 6/49, Lotto Max and Daily Grand. BCLC says "National game draws are all conducted in Toronto." ILC draws also cover the Lotto 6/49 Guaranteed Prize Draw (the Gold Ball) and Lotto Max MaxMillions.

Regional games are drawn separately. BCLC says it conducts its own regional draws, such as BC/49 and Extra, in Kamloops. WCLC's Lotto Spot help centre says WCLC conducts draws for its regional games, such as Western 649 and Western Max, in Winnipeg. WCLC's game and draw security page states that "all draws for national and regional games" use random number generator software.

Our guide to how Canada's lotto games work explains the games and prize structure. This guide is only about the draw.

From balls to software

The national games were not always drawn by software. In April 2019 the Interprovincial Lottery Corporation announced it would retire the lottery balls for Lotto Max and Lotto 6/49 on May 14 and move to a random number generator, according to a Lottery Post report of a CBC Radio interview with an Atlantic Lottery spokesperson. He said his corporation had been using the technology for its own regional games since 2017.

His stated reason was the audit trail: "We actually have two sets of auditors: one who is on-site and they can examine the results to make sure that everything went according to plan. And then we have another set of auditors in a completely different location who also checks the results to make sure that the results match." He added that the switch was about modernizing the process and keeping it "as random and secure as ever." Readers commenting on the report objected to the change. The corporations' answer is the auditing described below.

What "random number generator" means here

A random number generator (RNG) is software that produces numbers with no pattern that can be predicted. WCLC's security page describes its version this way:

  • The system "uses a proprietary algorithm to generate random numbers". It is "independently tested and certified for both its effectiveness in generating random results and its integrity."
  • "Before each draw, an integrity check is performed on the system to ensure that no changes have been made that could affect the random outcome of the draw."
  • Draws run on "stand-alone computerized draw machines" in a secure location "monitored by video surveillance 24 hours a day."
  • "All draws are witnessed by external auditors."

BCLC says the same of the national draws: the RNG software has been "tested and certified for randomness" by third-party experts, "including two draw auditors located in two separate geographical locations." Loto-Québec states that for its draw-based games the winning numbers "are chosen by a random number generator" and that every combination has the same odds of being drawn at every draw.

Lotto Max's game conditions, published through Atlantic Lottery, say ILC will cause the main numbers, MaxPlus series and MaxMillions series "to be drawn at random" from the numbers 1 to 52.

What the auditors do, step by step

WCLC's security page gives the most detailed public account of the checks around a draw. It covers WCLC's own system, and the national draws are run by ILC, whose procedures we could not find published in the same detail. The steps are still a useful picture of how a controlled lottery draw is built.

  1. Sales close. Each draw has a cut-off, the "game break", after which no further tickets can be sold for it. WCLC staff verify the cut-off and independent third-party auditors confirm it before the draw may go ahead.
  2. Tickets are copied. All tickets registered for that draw are copied to a file and transferred to an Independent Control System. This system is separate from the game system and is "under the exclusive control of the auditors". The auditors also receive a separate disc with the ticket information.
  3. The numbers are drawn. The RNG draw runs on a stand-alone machine, after its integrity check, with auditors present.
  4. Winners are found twice. Right after the draw, the winning numbers are entered into both the game system and the Independent Control System to identify the winners in each prize category.
  5. The two results are compared. The auditors verify that the results from the two systems are identical.

The point of steps 2, 4 and 5 is that the list of tickets is frozen and held outside the lottery's own system before the numbers exist. On our reading, that means a ticket added to the game system after the cut-off would show up as a difference when the auditors compare it with their independent copy.

BCLC adds that for national draws "Representatives from an independent firm audit every aspect of the draws to verify results and ensure their integrity." For its own regional draws, it says "regular external and internal audits check the physical security of the solution, which also runs self-tests to ensure integrity."

What Quebec's regulation says

Quebec is useful because its rules are public law, not just corporate descriptions. The Règlement sur les concours de pronostics et les jeux sur numéros, published on Légis Québec and current to July 1, 2026, covers Loto-Québec's number games. Several articles bear on integrity:

  • Article 14 says Loto-Québec determines winning numbers "by means of one or more drums that pick numbers at random, or a computer capable of generating random numbers." The regulation allows either method.
  • Article 8 says that if a ticket and the data held on Loto-Québec's computer differ, the computer data prevails.
  • Article 10 sets a floor on payout: the annual value of prizes offered cannot be less than 35% of total wagers for each lottery system.
  • Article 18 frees Loto-Québec of obligations on a prize if no claim notice arrives within a year after the draw.

Loto-Québec's responsible gambling site adds that draws "are conducted under the supervision of external auditors" and that tickets "are printed using strict procedures." The article summaries above are our own translations from the French text.

The official record decides

Because results are held electronically, the corporation's record matters. Quebec's article 8 says the computer data prevails over a printed ticket. WCLC's rules say that if a terminal-issued ticket shows entries that differ from the entries recorded in the computer system, the ticket "shall conclusively be deemed to bear the entries which are so recorded." Atlantic Lottery's claim page says that if the winning numbers it publishes ever differ from the official winning numbers report, "the latter will prevail."

The same WCLC rules let the corporation postpone or cancel a draw "in any circumstance where the integrity or normal operation of a lottery could be compromised." In practice, check your ticket on the corporation's own app, site or terminal, not a third-party site. That includes this one: our results pages and number checker are conveniences, not a source of record.

The second question: are prizes paid to the right person?

A fair draw doesn't help if a ticket is claimed by the wrong person. Ontario learned this the hard way. CBC News reported in 2026 that the Ontario ombudsman flagged suspicious insider wins in the mid-2000s, including a 2003 Super 7 jackpot of $12.5 million that was fraudulently claimed by three family members working at a convenience store, who were found guilty of stealing lottery tickets. A lottery lawyer told CBC that this kind of case "undermines confidence in the whole lottery corporation or the whole lottery scheme."

Today's controls on the claim side include ID checks, interviews for large prizes and the publicity described in our first 30 days guide. BCLC's winner publicity policy names "player trust and confidence in the integrity and security of BCLC's games" among its purposes, and says publicising winners shows that a prize "has been paid to the rightful person(s)." That is why large winners are named in public, and why anonymity is rare.

What the checks do and don't tell you

What they support

  • Each draw is independent. Loto-Québec says that if a number was drawn last week it "has exactly the same odds of being drawn the following week."
  • Every combination is equally likely. The odds don't depend on whether you pick your own numbers or a Quick Pick. Loto-Québec states this directly for Lotto 6/49: "Whether we play our lucky numbers or a Quick Pick makes no difference."
  • "Hot" and "cold" numbers are not signals. Loto-Québec calls the idea of hot or cold numbers a false belief, because draws "reset to zero every time." Our history of past draws describes the past only.

What we couldn't confirm

  • The algorithm and testing details. WCLC calls its algorithm "proprietary". The pages we reviewed don't name the firms that test and certify the software, publish test results or describe ILC's national draw procedures at the level of detail WCLC gives for its own. We have left out any claim that goes beyond what the corporations state.
  • Who the auditors are. BCLC says "an independent firm" audits the national draws. We did not find the firm named on the pages we checked.
  • Parallel draw arrangements. Some third-party sites say a parallel draw is also run in Quebec. We could not confirm that on a corporation page, so we have not included it.

Those gaps are worth knowing about. They are limits on what the public can verify, not evidence of a problem. If you want more detail, the corporations' security pages and your province's gaming regulator are the places to ask.

Quick answers

  • Is Lotto Max drawn by balls? No. ILC moved Lotto Max and Lotto 6/49 to a random number generator from mid-May 2019.
  • Where are the national draws held? In Toronto, according to BCLC.
  • Who watches? Independent auditors; BCLC says an independent firm audits every aspect of the draws.
  • How is the ticket list protected? WCLC describes a frozen copy of all tickets held by the auditors in a separate system and compared with the game system's results after the draw.
  • Can I trust a "system" that predicts numbers? No. The corporations describe the draws as random and independent. See our scams guide.
