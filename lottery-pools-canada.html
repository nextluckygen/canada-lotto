Office and group lottery pools in Canada: rules, risks and a sample agreement

Written by NextGen, British Columbia · Last updated October 2026

Lottery pools are common in Canadian workplaces, families and friend groups, and many large jackpots are won by groups. A pool is also where lottery money most often turns into an argument. This guide covers what the lottery corporations require when a group wins, what a 2025 British Columbia court decision shows about informal pools, and what a written agreement should cover. A sample agreement is included at the end.

Not legal advice. This is general information drawn from lottery corporation rules and news reports. Laws and corporation rules change. If a lot of money is at stake, or a dispute has started, talk to a lawyer in your province.

Why pools go wrong

Most pools run on trust and habit. Someone collects $5 or $10 from each person, buys tickets, and posts the numbers in a group chat. That works until a ticket wins. Then everyone asks the same questions at once:

  • Was this ticket bought with pool money, or with the organizer's own money?
  • Who was in the pool for this draw? Did the person who missed last week's payment still count?
  • Does a free ticket won by the pool belong to the pool?
  • Who holds the ticket, and who claims it?

Without written records, the answers come down to memory and goodwill. Both tend to fail when the prize is large.

A real case: the $2 million BC/49 dispute

Global News reported on January 14, 2025 that the B.C. Supreme Court had dismissed a lawsuit by a group of coworkers who said they were entitled to shares of a $2 million BC/49 prize won by a colleague. According to that report:

  • The coworkers said they had a pool and that the winning ticket was a pool ticket.
  • The court found no evidence that group money was used to buy the winning ticket, and no binding agreement to pool money on a regular basis. Justice Bantourakis described the arrangement as "ad hoc."
  • The winning ticket was bought for $10 with a debit card, which didn't match the roughly $50 group purchases the coworkers described.
  • The report noted BCLC's advice that groups set out written rules and keep good records.

The lesson isn't that pool members can never win in court. It's that a court decides based on evidence: what was agreed, who paid, and which tickets were bought with whose money. A written agreement and a simple record of each draw are the evidence.

What each lottery corporation requires from a group

Lottery corporations don't settle disputes between pool members. They pay the ticket holder, or a designated group representative, according to their own published process. Here is what each one says.

British Columbia: BCLC

  • BCLC defines a group prize as one where "three or more people have ownership on a lottery ticket."
  • The Group Trustee "is the purchaser and lawful holder of the ticket," is authorized to receive payment in trust for the members, and makes sure winnings are distributed according to each member's entitlement.
  • The trustee should mark the original ticket to show it belongs to a group, for example "In Trust" or "Group Ticket", and must sign it and fill out the back.
  • "Only the original ticket can be used to claim a group prize. Group copies of the ticket will not be accepted."
  • Group claims of $10,000 or more require a Group Prize Agreement completed by all members entitled to a share.

Prairies and the North: WCLC

WCLC, which runs lotteries in Alberta, Saskatchewan, Manitoba and the territories, requires groups claiming a prize to complete a Group Buying Agreement. For questions, WCLC Player Care is at 1-800-665-3313.

Ontario: OLG

OLG says group prize claims are submitted by the group leader. Its winner announcements regularly feature groups, which shows how often pools win in Ontario.

Atlantic Canada: Atlantic Lottery

  • For a group ticket, "the Group Trustee must sign and should indicate 'In Trust' on the ticket."
  • "Prizes will only be paid to the rightful ticket holder or Group Trustee."
  • For a group win of two or more people, "a Group Win Agreement Form must be completed by all members of the group."
  • For prizes of $25,000 or more, "if you are a member of a group, you will all be required to complete a Group Win Agreement," and winners are required to allow publicity of their name, city, prize and photo.

Quebec: Loto-Québec

Loto-Québec frequently announces prizes shared by groups. For example, the $70 million Lotto Max jackpot of February 25, 2020 was shared by a family of eight. We didn't find a Loto-Québec page describing its group claim form in enough detail to summarize, so check with Loto-Québec customer service (1-866-611-5686) before a claim.

What this means in practice

  • Pick the trustee in advance. In BC and Atlantic Canada, the trustee is the person who holds and signs the ticket. That should be agreed before anyone buys a ticket, not after a win.
  • Every member will likely need to sign. BCLC, WCLC and Atlantic Lottery all require a group form, and BCLC and Atlantic Lottery say all members complete it. A member who refuses to sign, or can't be reached, can hold up the claim.
  • Copies don't count. A photo of the ticket in the group chat is a useful record for members, but it can't be used to claim.

What a good pool agreement covers

A pool agreement doesn't need legal language. It needs to answer, in advance, the questions that come up after a win. These are the points that matter most:

  1. Members and shares. Who is in the pool, and whether shares are equal or based on how much each person paid.
  2. Contributions. How much each member pays, how often, and by when.
  3. Missed payments. The most common source of disputes. State plainly that a member who hasn't paid by the deadline isn't in that draw.
  4. The trustee. Who buys, holds and signs the tickets, and who makes the claim. Name a backup.
  5. Which tickets are pool tickets. Games, draws and number of tickets. Say that the trustee's personal tickets are kept separate.
  6. Records. Before each draw, the trustee shares a photo of the front and back of every pool ticket, with the list of members who paid.
  7. Small prizes and free plays. Whether small wins are paid out or reinvested, and that free tickets won by pool tickets belong to the pool.
  8. Large prizes. That everyone will sign the corporation's group form, and how the prize will be divided.
  9. Leaving and joining. How members leave, and when a new member's participation starts.
  10. Publicity. Corporations like Atlantic Lottery require publicity for large prizes. Members should know this before they join.

Sample lottery pool agreement

Adapt this to your group. Fill in the blanks, have every member sign and date it, and give everyone a copy. It isn't a lottery corporation form. When a group wins, you'll still need to complete the corporation's own group form.

LOTTERY POOL AGREEMENT

1. Pool name and start date. This agreement sets the rules for the ____________ lottery pool (the "Pool"), starting ____________, 20__.

2. Members. The members of the Pool are listed in the attached Member List, with each member's name, signature and date of joining.

3. Trustee. ____________ is the Trustee. The Trustee buys and holds all Pool tickets, marks each ticket "In Trust" or "Group Ticket" and signs it, and makes any prize claim on behalf of the Pool. If the Trustee is unavailable, ____________ is the Backup Trustee.

4. Contributions. Each member contributes $______ per ☐ draw ☐ week ☐ month, paid to the Trustee by ____________ (day and time) before each draw. Payment method: ____________.

5. Participation in a draw. Only members whose contribution has been received by the deadline in section 4 take part in that draw. The Trustee will not advance money for a member who hasn't paid.

6. Pool tickets. The Pool will buy ______ tickets for ____________ (game) for each draw, including these add-ons: ____________. Pool tickets are bought only with Pool money. Any tickets the Trustee buys for themselves are kept separate and are not Pool tickets.

7. Records. Before each draw, the Trustee will share with all members a clear photo of the front and back of every Pool ticket, and the list of members who paid for that draw. These records are the Pool's evidence of which tickets belong to it and who takes part.

8. Shares. Each participating member in a draw receives ☐ an equal share ☐ a share in proportion to their contribution, of any prize won by a Pool ticket from that draw.

9. Small prizes and free tickets. Prizes under $______ will be ☐ paid out ☐ used to buy Pool tickets for the next draw. Free tickets won by Pool tickets are Pool tickets for the members who took part in the winning draw.

10. Large prizes. If a Pool ticket wins a prize that the lottery corporation requires to be claimed as a group, every participating member agrees to complete and sign the corporation's group claim form promptly, and to provide the identification it requires. Members understand that the lottery corporation may publish winners' names, communities and photos.

11. Leaving the Pool. A member may leave by telling the Trustee in writing. They are not part of any draw after their last paid draw. New members take part from the first draw they pay for.

12. Changes. Changes to this agreement must be in writing and agreed by ☐ all members ☐ a majority of members.

13. Disputes. Members agree to try to resolve any disagreement among themselves first, using the records in section 7.

Running the pool week to week

  • Use a shared record. A simple spreadsheet with draw date, members paid, tickets bought and results is enough.
  • Post ticket photos before the draw, not after. A photo shared before the draw shows the ticket existed and was a pool ticket. One posted after a win proves much less.
  • Check tickets promptly. Draw-game prizes expire about a year after the draw. See unclaimed prizes and ticket expiry.
  • Keep the cost reasonable. Make it easy for people to say no or leave. A lottery pool should be optional, especially at work. Lotteries are a form of entertainment with long odds; see our odds calculator.

Questions

Do we need a written agreement for the lottery corporation to pay us?

The corporations have their own group forms that you complete at claim time. A pool agreement is separate. It's for the members, so they agree on the rules and have evidence if there's a disagreement.

Can one person claim for the whole group?

The trustee or group leader handles the claim, but BCLC, WCLC and Atlantic Lottery all describe group forms completed by the members. Plan for every member to be involved.

What if the trustee bought the winning ticket with their own money?

This is exactly what the B.C. case turned on. According to Global News, the court found no evidence that group money was used, and the claim failed. Clear records of which tickets were bought with pool money settle that question in advance.
