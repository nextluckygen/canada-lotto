Who runs Lotto Max and Lotto 6/49 in Canada

Lotto Max and Lotto 6/49 in Canada are run jointly by the provincial lottery corporations through the Interprovincial Lottery Corporation. Lotto 6/49 and Lotto Max are national games. You buy a ticket from a retailer or online platform licensed in your province or territory, the draw is the same across the country, and prize claims follow the rules of the corporation that issued your ticket.

Who is who

The Interprovincial Lottery Corporation (ILC) coordinates the national games on behalf of the regional lottery corporations. Depending on where you live, the name on your ticket will be one of:

  • BCLC — British Columbia (online play through PlayNow.com);
  • WCLC (Western Canada Lottery Corporation) — Alberta, Saskatchewan, Manitoba, Yukon, the Northwest Territories and Nunavut;
  • OLG — Ontario;
  • Loto-Québec — Quebec;
  • Atlantic Lottery — New Brunswick, Nova Scotia, Prince Edward Island, and Newfoundland and Labrador.

WCLC's public winning-number pages are a convenient and well-structured official source for both national games, which is why this site's results archive reads them. lottohelper.ca is none of these organisations. We do not print tickets, take payment, hold a prize pool or decide winners.

A short history of the national games

National lotto in Canada grew out of co-operation between the provinces. According to Loto-Québec's corporate history, Ontario and the western provinces set up the Interprovincial Lottery Corporation in 1976, and Quebec and the Atlantic provinces joined in 1978. OLG has also described the ILC as founded in 1976 by the provincial lottery organizations and based in Toronto.

Lotto 6/49 is the oldest game still running. The first draw in our archive is from June 12, 1982, and the game has used the same 1-to-49 number range ever since. For its first three years it was drawn once a week, on Saturdays. The first Wednesday draw in the records is September 11, 1985, and since then there have been about 104 or 105 draws a year. By October 24, 2026 the archive held 4,462 Lotto 6/49 draws.

Super 7 was launched in 1994 as the national "big jackpot" game. It was replaced by Lotto Max: Super 7's last draw was on September 18, 2009, and the first Lotto Max draw followed on September 25, 2009, with a $10 million jackpot. Lotto Max has been changed several times since:

  • 2009: seven numbers from 1 to 49, drawn on Friday nights, $5 for three selections, and a $50 million jackpot cap with $1 million MaxMillions prizes above it (OLG launch release).
  • 2015: the maximum jackpot rose to $60 million (OLG, 2019).
  • May 2019: the number range grew to 1 to 50, a Tuesday draw was added (first held May 14, 2019), and the maximum jackpot rose to $70 million (BCLC and OLG announcements).
  • April 2026: the range grew to 1 to 52, the price rose to $6 for four selections, the jackpot range became $10 million to $90 million, and MaxPlus prizes were added. The first 1-to-52 draw was on April 14, 2026.

Lotto 6/49 has also been redesigned. The current version has a fixed $5 million Classic Draw jackpot and a separate Gold Ball draw with a growing jackpot. Our Gold Ball guide explains how it works. Because these rule changes alter the odds and the prize tables, our results pages keep each Lotto Max number-range era separate. For what the full record shows, see 44 years of draws, by the numbers.

Draw nights

  • Lotto 6/49: Wednesday and Saturday
  • Lotto Max: Tuesday and Friday

In Western Canada, ticket sales for draw-night games close at 7:30 p.m. Pacific (8:30 p.m. Mountain, 9:30 p.m. Central) on the day of the draw, according to WCLC. Holiday changes are announced by the operators. This site only adds a result after the published numbers pass a date-and-count check.

What you are buying

  • Lotto 6/49 ($3): one six-number selection from 1 to 49 for the Classic Draw (fixed $5 million jackpot), plus a computer-generated Gold Ball number for the Gold Ball draw. Details: Gold Ball guide.
  • Lotto Max ($6, since April 2026): four selections of seven numbers from 1 to 52 — you choose one and three are Quick Picks — with jackpots from $10 million to $90 million plus MaxPlus and MaxMillions prizes. Details: Lotto Max guide.
  • Add-ons: Extra (BC, Prairies, territories) and Encore (Ontario) are separate $1 games with their own draws and prize tables, and they differ by region. Details: Extra and Encore explained.

Where to check and where to claim

Check tickets with your operator's app or a retailer's self-serve ticket checker, which reads the barcode and covers every game on the ticket. Small prizes are usually paid at retail, subject to the retailer's cash on hand; larger prizes go through claim centres or offices with identification. Limits differ by province — our claims guide for BC and Western Canada has the details. Never send a photo of your ticket to anyone who contacts you after a draw.

Online play

Some provinces sell official tickets online through the operator's own platform — PlayNow.com in British Columbia and Manitoba, PlayAlberta.ca in Alberta and OLG.ca in Ontario, for example. That is different from offshore "lottery ticket" websites, which are not your provincial corporation and operate under different (or no) Canadian rules. This website will never add a buy button.

Where the money goes

Only part of each ticket price goes into prizes. For Lotto Max, WCLC says the prize fund is at least 48% of sales. The rest pays retailer commissions, ticket printing, terminals, draw operations and advertising, and what is left becomes provincial revenue. Each province decides how to use that revenue. In British Columbia, BCLC says its net income goes to the Province. Of the $1.5 billion generated in 2024, $1 billion funded public programs and services including education, $147.3 million went to the Provincial Health Special Account, $140 million went to Community Gaming Grants, and $94.5 million went to local governments and First Nations that host gaming facilities. Other provinces have their own arrangements.

The prize fund is divided into tiers. Lower tiers such as Free Play or $20 are usually fixed. Higher "Pools Fund" tiers are pari-mutuel: the money set aside for that tier is split among everyone in the country who wins it. That is why a second-prize amount can be very different from one draw to the next. Unwon jackpot money rolls over, which is how jackpots grow. On Lotto Max, it also pays for MaxMillions and MaxPlus prizes. What happens when the jackpot hits its ceiling is covered in what happens at the $90 million cap.

How a national draw reaches your ticket

Every ticket sold in Canada for a given draw goes into the same national pool. Sales close in every region before the draw. In Western Canada, WCLC says that happens at 7:30 p.m. Pacific, and its Lotto Max page puts the national cutoff at 10:30 p.m. Eastern. The numbers are drawn once for the whole country. The regional corporations then publish the results and prize breakdowns, and their terminals and apps start paying claims.

Because the pool is national but the claim process is regional, two winners of the same prize in different provinces can have quite different experiences. A $5,000 winner in Vancouver can claim online or at a BCLC Regional Prize Payout location. The same win in Calgary goes to a WCLC prize office. Prize amounts themselves are identical across the country.

Common misunderstandings

  • "Buying in a big city gives better odds." Every ticket has exactly the same chance, wherever it is sold. Cities simply sell more tickets, so they produce more winners.
  • "Quick Picks win less." Quick Picks and chosen numbers win in proportion to how many are sold. The draw does not know how a line was chosen.
  • "Lottery winnings are taxed in Canada." Prizes from Canadian lotteries are generally not taxable income. Income you later earn on the money is. See are lottery winnings taxed in Canada?
  • "You can claim anywhere." You claim from the corporation printed on the back of your ticket, under its rules and deadlines.
