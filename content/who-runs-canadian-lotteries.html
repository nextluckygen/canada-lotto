Who runs lotteries in Canada: the provinces, the ILC and where ticket money goes

There is no federal lottery in Canada. Lotto Max, Lotto 6/49 and Daily Grand are run by five provincial lottery corporations that work together through a shared body, the Interprovincial Lottery Corporation. This guide explains the legal basis for that arrangement, who does what, and what the corporations themselves say about where a ticket dollar goes. It also shows why the published figures can't be compared one-to-one.

What this guide is. A plain-language summary of laws, rules and annual reports. It describes how the system is organised. It doesn't say anything about your chances of winning, which depend only on the game's published odds. See Lotto Max vs 6/49 vs Daily Grand for those.

Why provinces run lotteries

Gambling in Canada is governed by the federal Criminal Code, which makes most gaming and betting illegal unless an exception applies. The exception for lotteries is section 207. Under 207(1)(a) it is lawful "for the government of a province, either alone or in conjunction with the government of another province, to conduct and manage a lottery scheme in that province, or in that and the other province, in accordance with any law enacted by the legislature of that province". Charities, fairs and a few other licensed operators have their own, narrower exceptions in the same section.

The phrase "in conjunction with the government of another province" is what allows national games. Section 207(1)(e) also lets one province agree that tickets for a lottery run in another province may be sold in its own.

The 1985 agreement

The provinces didn't always hold this power alone. A Library of Parliament paper on the history of the Criminal Code's gambling rules describes a June 1985 agreement between Ottawa and the provinces. The federal government agreed to amend the Criminal Code so that provinces and their agencies had the sole legal right to conduct lotteries. In return, the provinces agreed to pay $100 million, which went toward Canada's commitment to the 1988 Winter Olympics in Calgary. They also reaffirmed annual payments of $24 million, adjusted for inflation, under a 1979 agreement, each province paying a share in proportion to its lottery sales. The Senate's legal committee has described the same arrangement. The amendment became law in December 1985.

That 1979 agreement still shows up in the books. In its financial statements for the year ended March 31, 2025, Ontario's lottery corporation notes that the provincial lottery corporations make payments to the Government of Canada under the August 1979 agreement, which "stipulates that the Government of Canada will not participate in the sale of lottery tickets."

The five regional corporations

Each province's lottery business is handled by a provincial corporation or a group of provinces sharing one. The Interprovincial Lottery Corporation's rules list them by region:

CorporationTerritory it serves
BCLC (British Columbia Lottery Corporation)British Columbia
WCLC (Western Canada Lottery Corporation)Alberta, Manitoba, Saskatchewan, Yukon, the Northwest Territories and Nunavut
OLG (Ontario Lottery and Gaming Corporation)Ontario
Loto-QuébecQuebec
Atlantic Lottery CorporationNew Brunswick, Newfoundland and Labrador, Nova Scotia and Prince Edward Island

The rules call each of these a "Regional Marketing Organization". A corporation may sell only in its own region. BCLC's FAQ puts it plainly: it "is authorized to market and distribute lottery tickets within B.C. only." Atlantic Lottery's own explainer says each of the five is regulated to deliver products in its jurisdiction only.

What the Interprovincial Lottery Corporation does

The Interprovincial Lottery Corporation (ILC) is the body through which the five corporations run the games they share. A 2019 contract announcement from Pollard Banknote, a ticket printer, describes the ILC as incorporated in 1976 and as the coordinating agent for the five lottery corporations, which "oversee the planning, risk management, and business oversight of national initiatives through the ILC Board, its Executive Committee, and other specific committees and working groups."

In practice that means:

  • The national draw games. BCLC says it manages and markets Lotto 6/49, Lotto Max and Daily Grand "in conjunction with" the ILC. The ILC conducts those draws in Toronto. Our guide to how draws are run and audited covers that process.
  • Some instant tickets. Pollard's announcement says it provides Instant tickets and game design to the five corporations through the ILC. Some scratch games are therefore national, printed once and sold in every region. Our guide to scratch tickets and lotto draws explains how to read them.
  • A common rulebook. The ILC's Rules and Regulations Respecting Lotteries and Lottery Tickets, adopted January 26, 2024, apply to the national lotteries. They define a lottery as a lottery scheme "conducted and managed by the governments of the provinces through the Corporation and Regional Marketing Organizations."

Each corporation runs its own regional games on its own. BCLC says BC/49 and Extra are drawn at its Kamloops office, and WCLC says its regional games such as Western 649 and Western Max are drawn in Winnipeg. These are separate from the national draws. Our guide to Extra and Encore explains the add-on games.

One game, five computer systems

The split between ILC and regional corporations has a practical effect on winners. WCLC's FAQ says that for the national games, "each region maintains its own computer system and database of winning tickets, so terminals in a region can only verify tickets purchased in their own region." To claim a prize on a ticket bought elsewhere, you return to the region of purchase or mail the ticket to the address on the back. BCLC says the same: "Lottery winnings must be claimed from the lottery jurisdiction that sold the ticket." The ILC's rules also say a Regional Marketing Organization may record online ticket orders only from residents of its own region. Our guide to claiming a prize province by province has the details.

The same structure explains what happens to unclaimed prizes. BCLC says unclaimed prizes from national games are returned to players through future bonus games or promotions and are held separately, administered by the ILC. Unclaimed prizes from games run only by BCLC are counted in its net income and passed to the provincial government. WCLC says the same split applies in its region. See unclaimed lottery prizes.

Who oversees the corporations

These are government-owned businesses, so they answer to their provinces. Ontario's lottery corporation is created by statute, the Ontario Lottery and Gaming Corporation Act, 1999, whose stated purposes include "to generate revenues for the Province" and "to promote responsible gaming with respect to lottery schemes." BCLC says that all of its retailers, game suppliers and vendors are certified by British Columbia's Independent Gambling Control Office. Regulatory arrangements differ by province, and we haven't reviewed each one. If you want to know who oversees lotteries where you live, your provincial government's gaming pages are the place to start.

Where the money goes: what each corporation says

Every corporation reports prizes, retailer commissions, operating costs and a return to government. The way each one presents those figures differs, so we show them separately rather than in one table.

Western Canada Lottery Corporation

WCLC's FAQ gives a breakdown per dollar of ticket sales. Its FAQ page doesn't carry a date, so treat these as approximate and check the current page:

Share of sales (WCLC FAQ)Goes to
About 52.3%Players, as prizes
About 6.6%Lottery retailers, as commissions
About 5.5%WCLC operating expenses
About 1.1%Ticket printing
About 34.1%Revenue returned to WCLC's members, the governments of Alberta, Saskatchewan and Manitoba, plus associate members Yukon, the Northwest Territories and Nunavut

WCLC adds that revenue is based on sales from each member province or territory, and that "each provincial and territorial government establishes its own priorities for disbursement of lottery funds."

Ontario Lottery and Gaming Corporation

Ontario's law sets an order of payment. Under section 14 of the OLG Act, the corporation pays from its revenue, in order of priority: prizes and winnings to players; payments required by regulation to the Consolidated Revenue Fund; operating expenses; payments to the Alcohol and Gaming Commission of Ontario; payments under the First Nations revenue-sharing agreement; and certain payments supporting live horse racing. After that, any remaining revenue is paid into the Consolidated Revenue Fund.

OLG's audited statements for the year ended March 31, 2025 show lottery numbers in a separate column. In its revenue breakdown, the retail lottery line had proceeds of $4,149 million, with lottery prizes of $2,443 million. Retailer commissions were $312 million. Our arithmetic from those figures:

  • Prizes: $2,443 million of $4,149 million is about 58.9%.
  • Retailer commissions: $312 million of $4,149 million is about 7.5%.

Two cautions. The commissions figure in the statements may not map perfectly onto the sales line, so treat 7.5% as a rough guide. And OLG's retail line includes sports products as well as lotto and instant tickets, so this is not a lotto-only figure. News coverage quotes an OLG spokesperson saying retailers earn a 5% commission on online-ticket sales and 8% on Instant tickets, plus a smaller commission on redeemed prizes and occasional promotions. We couldn't confirm those rates on an OLG page, so treat them as reported rather than official.

Atlantic Lottery

Atlantic Lottery's 2024-25 annual report says it returned all of its profit, $498.7 million, to its four provincial shareholders: $165.1 million to Nova Scotia, $154.6 million to New Brunswick, $153.1 million to Newfoundland and Labrador and $25.9 million to Prince Edward Island. Its audited statements say it "does not retain any earnings."

The report also gives a "where the money goes" breakdown per lottery dollar: 37 cents of profit to Atlantic Canada, 34 cents to winners, 9 cents to retailers, 8 cents to operations, 4 cents to salaries and benefits, 4 cents to operations outside the region and 4 cents to taxes. Those figures cover the whole business, which also includes its casino ("Destination") line. They aren't a lottery-only split and don't match the WCLC breakdown above. The statements also show payments to the Government of Canada under the 1979 agreement of $5.4 million for the year.

BCLC and Loto-Québec

BCLC's lottery page says it paid out $813 million in lottery prizes in 2025/26, that it owns and manages the lottery equipment in 3,600+ retail locations, and that "proceeds from lottery games benefit people and communities throughout British Columbia." We couldn't open a lottery-only profit figure from BCLC's published reports and haven't included one. We also didn't find a Loto-Québec breakdown we could verify against its own report, so we have left that out rather than quote a secondary source.

Why you can't compare the percentages directly

  • Different scopes. WCLC's figures are shares of lottery sales. OLG's statements combine lottery, casinos, charitable gaming and online gaming. Atlantic Lottery's per-dollar figures include casino operations.
  • Different products. Instant tickets and lotto draws return different shares as prizes. A corporation with more instant sales will show a different mix.
  • Different timing. One page may be undated, another covers the year to March 2025, another the year to March 2026.
  • Different definitions. Some corporations count free tickets as prizes, some net prizes against sales first, and some report commissions inside a larger "fees" line.

What you can say is simple. Roughly half or more of lottery sales go back as prizes in the figures published above, a small share goes to retailers, and the rest, after operating costs, goes to government. The exact shares vary by corporation and game. None of this changes the odds of a given ticket, and on average a ticket costs more than it returns. If you play, set a budget first.

What this means for you as a player

  • Buy where you live. Your region's corporation sells to you, validates your ticket and pays your prize.
  • Keep the ticket's region in mind. A national-game ticket from another province can only be claimed through the corporation that sold it.
  • Be wary of anyone outside this system. Real lottery notices come from your regional corporation about a ticket you bought. See our scams guide, and our guide to buying tickets online by province for the official sellers.
  • Read the claim period. It is set by the corporation and game, not by the ILC alone. WCLC says national and regional draw tickets in its region expire one year from the draw date.

Quick answers

  • Is there a national lottery run by Ottawa? No. Since the 1985 agreement, the Criminal Code gives provinces, alone or together, the authority to run lotteries.
  • Who runs Lotto Max? The five regional corporations through the ILC, which conducts the draw in Toronto.
  • Where does a ticket's profit go? To the provincial governments that own the corporations, which each decide how to spend it.
  • Can I use a BC ticket to claim in Ontario? No. Claim through the corporation that sold the ticket.
