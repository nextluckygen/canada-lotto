Lump sum vs annuity: how Canadian lottery prizes are paid

Ask an American lottery winner how they want to be paid and they face a real choice: 30 annual payments or a much smaller cash amount up front. Ask a Lotto Max winner in Canada and there's nothing to decide. The advertised jackpot is paid in one cheque, and it isn't taxed. This guide explains why, the one Canadian game where an annuity choice exists, and what the difference means for the money.

The short version

  • Lotto Max and Lotto 6/49: prizes are paid as a single lump sum. The advertised jackpot is the amount the winner receives, or splits with other winners.
  • No income tax on the prize: the Canada Revenue Agency does not treat lottery winnings as taxable income. Interest, dividends or capital gains earned on the money afterwards are taxable.
  • Daily Grand is the exception: its top prizes are advertised "for life", and winners can choose an annuity or a lump sum.
  • US jackpots are different: the headline Powerball figure is the total of a 30-payment annuity. The cash option is smaller, and both are taxed.

What "lump sum" and "annuity" mean

A lump sum is a single payment of the whole prize. You receive the money at once and decide how to spend, save or invest it.

An annuity is a series of payments over time: yearly, monthly, or daily in Daily Grand's advertising. The organization paying the prize usually funds it by buying a financial product that pays out on schedule. A dollar paid 20 years from now is worth less than a dollar today, because today's dollar could be invested in the meantime, so the cost of funding an annuity is much less than the sum of its payments. That gap is why the "cash value" of a US annuity jackpot is so much lower than the advertised figure.

Why Lotto Max and 6/49 jackpots are paid all at once

Canada's national lotto games were designed around a single payment. When a Lotto Max jackpot is advertised at $50 million, that's the amount the prize fund holds for the top prize. A sole winner receives $50 million. Two winners each receive $25 million. There is no hidden discount, and no option to spread it out. The same applies to MaxMillions and MaxPlus prizes, the Lotto 6/49 Classic Draw jackpot and the Gold Ball jackpot.

The lump sum also fits Canada's tax rules. Because the prize itself isn't taxed, there's no tax advantage to spreading it over many years, as there can be in countries that tax winnings as income. A Canadian winner who wants a steady income can create one by putting the lump sum into investments or buying an annuity from an insurer. They keep control of the capital and can choose the product.

Why the prize isn't taxed

The Canada Revenue Agency lists lottery winnings among amounts that are generally not taxable. Its income tax folio on prizes explains that a prize won in a lottery scheme is not income from employment or business, so it isn't taxed the way wages or self-employment income are. That holds whether you win $20 or $70 million, and there's no withholding when the cheque is issued.

What is taxable is what the money earns once it's yours. Interest on a savings account, dividends from shares and capital gains on investments are reported on your return in the normal way, starting from the day you invest. Our guide to whether lottery winnings are taxed in Canada goes into the details, including gifts and what happens if you live outside Canada.

Daily Grand: the Canadian game with an annuity choice

Daily Grand is a national game advertised with "for life" prizes rather than a jackpot amount. According to WCLC:

PrizeAnnuity optionLump-sum option
Grand Prize (5 numbers + Grand Number)$1,000 a day for life$7,000,000
Second Prize (5 numbers)$25,000 a year for life$500,000

WCLC's Daily Grand FAQ and game conditions set out how the choice works:

  • The annuity is paid for the winner's lifetime with a minimum of 20 years. If the winner dies within the first 20 years, the remaining payments, or their commuted value, go to the winner's estate or beneficiaries.
  • The annuity is provided through a third-party financial provider arranged by the lottery corporations.
  • If a prize is shared, the winners split the lump-sum amount. The for-life option is for a sole winner.
  • If a winner does not choose, the lump sum is deemed to have been chosen.

There's one tax detail. WCLC says lottery prizes are tax-free in Canada, "but there are tax implications if a DAILY GRAND winner chooses the annuity option." The prize stays tax-free, but an annuity grows over time, and that growth is investment income. WCLC adds that the annuity payments "include an additional amount to help offset tax amounts". Anyone facing this choice should ask a tax professional to run the numbers for their situation.

Is $1,000 a day better than $7 million?

There's no general answer. It depends on the winner's age, health, other income and how they would invest a lump sum. Some simple arithmetic helps frame it. $1,000 a day is about $365,000 a year. Over the 20-year minimum that's about $7.3 million, close to the $7 million lump sum, so the annuity only pays clearly more if the winner lives well beyond 20 years. On the other hand, a $7 million lump sum invested carefully could produce a meaningful income of its own while the capital stays in the winner's hands to leave to family. That's why the annuity's guarantee and the lump sum's flexibility are worth comparing with professional advice. These figures ignore inflation, investment returns and tax, and they are not advice.

How the US system differs

US jackpots are where most people first meet the annuity-versus-cash idea, and comparing them with Canadian figures can mislead. Powerball's official FAQ says the advertised jackpot is the annuity value: 30 graduated payments over 29 years, each 5% larger than the last. Winners can instead take a cash option, which is roughly what the prize pool holds today and so much less than the headline number. Both are paid before federal and often state income taxes are withheld.

So an "$800 million" US jackpot taken as cash, after tax, leaves a winner with a fraction of that figure. A $70 million Lotto Max jackpot is $70 million in the bank. The two numbers aren't comparable, and US-style figures in Canadian adverts for "international lotteries" are a common scam hook. See lottery scams in Canada.

If you win: practical first steps

Whichever way a prize is paid, the steps that protect it are the same:

  1. Sign the ticket and keep it safe. You have one year from the draw to claim in BC and the WCLC provinces. There's no need to rush.
  2. Claim through official channels. In BC, prizes of $25,000 or more are claimed by appointment at BCLC's Kamloops or Vancouver office and paid by cheque. See how to claim a prize in BC.
  3. Park the money somewhere simple. A high-interest savings account or a short-term deposit at a federally regulated institution gives you time to think. Remember the interest is taxable.
  4. Get fee-only advice. A planner paid by the hour, not by commission on products, and an accountant can help you build an income plan, perhaps including an insurer's annuity, without giving up control of the whole sum.
  5. Expect contact. Large winners often hear from scammers posing as officials, charities or "prize processors." The lottery corporation will never ask you for a fee to release a prize.

Frequently asked questions

Can I turn a Lotto Max lump sum into a regular income?

Yes, you can do that yourself. A winner can put part of the prize into investments or buy an annuity from an insurance company, choosing the terms, while the rest of the money stays under their control.

Do I pay tax on a US lottery prize if I live in Canada?

Foreign lottery prizes are treated differently, and US withholding rules can apply to non-residents. That depends on the specific prize and the tax treaty, so ask a cross-border tax professional. The tax-free rule described here is about prizes from Canadian lotteries.

Is the Daily Grand lump sum taxed?

No. Like other Canadian lottery prizes, the lump sum is not taxable income. Income earned on it afterwards is taxable.
