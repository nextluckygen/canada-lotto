Your first 30 days after a big lottery win in Canada

Almost every guide to winning the lottery says "talk to a lawyer and an accountant". Few explain what actually happens in the first month in Canada: how the ticket is protected, why you will probably be named in public, what the CRA says about tax, and what changes if you won as part of a group. This guide goes through those steps using the lottery corporations' own rules, the CRA and news reports, and says plainly where a point is our suggestion rather than a published rule.

General information, not legal, tax or financial advice. Rules differ by province and change. For a prize large enough to matter, a lawyer and a financial professional licensed in your province should look at your actual situation. Nothing here is a reason to buy tickets; the odds are the same on every draw. See our odds calculator.

The short version

  • You usually have a year, not a day. WCLC's rules, Loto-Québec's regulation and the other corporations in our claiming guide all set a one-year limit from the draw for national games. That gives you time to plan, but not unlimited time.
  • Secure the ticket first. Sign it, copy both sides, and keep it somewhere safe. Share the news with as few people as possible until you have advice.
  • Anonymity is rare. Every corporation we checked either requires publicity for large prizes or reserves the right to use your name, city and photo. Exceptions are rare and need evidence of a safety risk.
  • The prize itself isn't taxed. The CRA says lottery winnings of any amount do not have to be reported. Interest and other income the money earns afterwards is taxable.
  • Groups need paperwork. Every corporation asks for a group agreement or a named group leader.

Days 1 to 3: protect the ticket and slow down

Confirm the win through an official channel

Check the ticket with the lottery corporation's app, website or a retailer's terminal. Our number checker compares a line with past draws, but only the corporation's own result counts. Atlantic Lottery's claim page states that if its published numbers differ from the official winning numbers report, "the latter will prevail". Loto-Québec's regulation and WCLC's rules both say the lottery's computer record decides if a printed ticket and the record ever differ.

Sign it and copy it

Atlantic Lottery tells players to sign the ticket in ink and fill in the back with name, address and a daytime phone number, which it says "helps to protect you if your ticket is lost or stolen". OLG and BCLC give similar advice, and WCLC asks for your name on the front and in the contact section on the back. Photograph or photocopy both sides, but keep the original safe. WCLC's rules say it has no obligation to pay unless the holder satisfies it that they are lawfully entitled to the ticket, and OLG says it knows when and where a ticket was sold but not who bought it. As an OLG spokesperson told CBC News in 2017, "It is anonymous play."

Don't announce it yet

Randy Rush, who won $50 million on Lotto Max in Alberta in 2015, told CBC News in 2026 that he left town quickly after collecting the prize. He said he was careful from the start, and that "there were a few years where it really took some time to weed people out, and some of them were very unpleasant." Ottawa financial planner Stephan Desbiens, who has advised lottery winners, told CBC he advises winners to keep the news to themselves as much as possible until they work out how they will handle the money. In his words, "once you go there and claim your prize, you don't have control over what happens next in terms of your privacy."

That is a planner's view, not a rule, but it fits how the claim process works: the moment you claim a large prize, the corporation will publicise it (see below). Our suggestion is to use the days before you claim to get advice and to decide who you will tell.

Days 3 to 10: get professional advice before you claim

OLG says it can't advise winners. Its media relations manager told CBC News: "Make sure you get a trusted lawyer, a trusted financial advisor. We can't give financial advice." He added that many winners arrange both before they come in to claim. A few points on what each does, offered as our suggestions:

  • A lawyer can review a group arrangement, deal with any dispute over who owns the ticket, and help with a request for privacy before you claim (see the next section).
  • A financial planner or advisor can set out where the money goes while you decide what to do with it, and what a plan for the first few years could look like.
  • A tax professional can confirm the CRA position below as it applies to you, and explain how the income your money earns will be reported.

Check each person's licence with the provincial regulator, and be wary of anyone who approaches you first. Our scams guide covers the fake "claim fee" and "tax" demands that target winners, and the advance-fee tricks aimed at people who haven't won at all.

A cautionary example from the same CBC story: Rush later co-authored two books about his experience, and the downsides he describes included "a lengthy legal battle with a former friend and business partner after Rush invested millions in his tech startup."

Days 7 to 14: make the claim, and expect publicity

Can you stay anonymous?

Almost never. BCLC says it considers requests "on a case by case basis, but the exceptions are rare", and its policy requires that a request be supported by evidence that can be independently confirmed, such as a serious threat to the winner's safety. An OLG spokesperson told CP24 in 2023 that anonymity had been granted only "a few" times, with criteria such as a battered spouse, undercover law enforcement or potential harm, and that a lawyer should contact OLG first. A Toronto lottery lawyer told CBC that general worry about scams "is not going to meet the test."

The corporations give the same reason: showing that the prize went to the right person. BCLC's policy lists transparency and "player trust and confidence in the integrity and security of BCLC's games" among its purposes. Ontario's ombudsman flagged suspicious insider wins in the mid-2000s, including a 2003 Super 7 prize of $12.5 million fraudulently claimed by three family members who worked at a convenience store (CBC), and publicity is part of how the corporations now show that claims are genuine. Our guide to how draws are audited covers the draw side of that integrity story.

Practical steps if you want to keep your profile low

  • Read the publicity wording on the claim form before you sign it. It differs by corporation.
  • Decide in advance what you'll say about the win and who you will tell privately.
  • Remember that a web story stays online. OLG told CBC that winners had raised exactly this concern, which is why it moved to first name and last initial in releases. The full name still appears on its website for 30 days.

Days 10 to 20: if you won with a group

Group wins are common, and they are where most disputes start. OLG says group claims must be made by a group leader, WCLC asks for a Group Buying Agreement, and Atlantic Lottery requires every member to sign a Group Win Agreement. If there is no written agreement, a court decides on evidence of who paid and what was agreed, as in the 2025 B.C. case described in our lottery pools guide.

OLG's advice to groups, as given to CBC: choose a captain, keep the ticket and a copy of the receipt available to everyone, and keep proof of who paid in. "Mark it down, send an email, make sure you have the documentation to say you have paid into the group."

On tax, the CRA folio says that where the winner is a syndicate, "the income tax consequences to each member of the syndicate are the same." So the CRA applies the same lottery-prize treatment to every member of a winning group.

What the CRA says about tax

The CRA's page on amounts that are not reported or taxed lists "lottery winnings of any amount, unless the prize can be considered income from employment, a business or property, or a prize for achievement". Its income tax folio on lottery winnings says a prize from a lottery scheme "is not taxable as either a capital gain or income." Our tax guide goes into more detail.

Three details matter in the first month:

  1. What the money earns is taxable. The CRA's page says: "any interest that you earn when you invest lottery winnings must be reported on your return." The same note covers every item on that list, including "most gifts and inheritances", so if you give money to family, ask your tax professional how income earned on it is reported.
  2. Frequent play is not a business. The folio says gambling may be taxable business income only in exceptional cases, and notes a court's view that regular, frequent play "is something that by its nature is not generally regarded as a commercial activity except under very exceptional circumstances."
  3. Keep records from day one. The date and amount of the payment, and the account it went into, make it simple to show later which money is the prize and which is income earned on it.

One payment choice interacts with tax. Lotto Max and Lotto 6/49 pay one cheque, while Daily Grand offers a for-life option. Our lump sum vs annuity guide covers how those payments work. The CRA folio has a separate paragraph on annuities as prizes, which is one more reason to see a tax professional before choosing.

Days 14 to 30: park the money before you decide what to do with it

This section is our suggestion, not a rule, but two published facts shape it.

  • Deposit insurance has limits. The Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation says each category of deposit is insured separately up to $100,000, including principal and interest, at each member institution, and that depositors with deposits in more than one category can be covered for more than $100,000 in total. CDIC says mutual funds, stocks, bonds and ETFs are not eligible. CDIC notes that proposed changes to its framework have not yet taken effect, so check its site for current rules before relying on the figures.
  • Tax-free accounts have annual limits. The CRA lists the 2026 TFSA dollar limit as $7,000. Your own contribution room depends on your age and history, so check it with the CRA before contributing, and ask your adviser what suits your situation.

Some other habits worth having by day 30:

  • Treat every approach as a possible scam. A genuine claim goes through the lottery corporation that sold the ticket, and our scams guide lists the signs of fake "fees" and "taxes".
  • Hold off on big commitments. Loans to friends, business investments and property purchases can wait. Nothing in the claim rules requires an early decision on any of them.
  • Check the deadline once more. If you haven't claimed yet, the one-year limit runs from the draw date, not from the date you learned of the win. Our unclaimed prizes guide shows what happens to tickets that miss it.

A 30-day checklist

Timing varies with the prize size, the corporation and your own circumstances. Treat this as an order of steps, not a schedule.

  • Day 1: check the ticket officially, sign it, copy both sides, store it securely, tell almost no one.
  • Days 2 to 7: if you bought with others, write down who is in the group and who will lead the claim. Find a lawyer and a financial professional.
  • Days 7 to 14: read the claim form's publicity wording with your lawyer. Book the claim appointment or file the online form. Bring ID that matches the name on the ticket.
  • Days 14 to 21: receive payment, then put it somewhere safe while you plan, keeping CDIC limits in mind.
  • Days 21 to 30: agree a first-year plan with your advisers, keep records for your tax professional, and decide how you will answer questions from family and friends.

What we couldn't confirm

We did not find an official source for the exact ID or interview thresholds at Loto-Québec, so we left them out. We also relied on news reports, not corporation pages, for OLG's current publicity wording, because OLG's own pages weren't available to our checks. Treat OLG details as reported, and read the claim form itself before you sign.
